# Idea Check: "has my idea been done, what is close to it, where is the gap?"

Status: researched 2026-10-18. **M1 built 2026-10-19** (the search plan and the closest papers). **M2 built 2026-10-22** (compare with each part of the idea, quotes checked). **M3 built 2026-10-22** (gaps, what was tried, an answer, a report; see "M3 as built"). Not yet done: calibration on RINoBench, open-access full text, saved history.

**M1 as built:** `/idea` reads the idea in the browser (PDF, Word, LaTeX, Markdown, text); the private model proposes a plan (title, summary, four facets, a paragraph to search by meaning, 3 to 6 keyword searches) with
identifying details left out and e-mail addresses and links removed; the researcher edits it; OpenAlex (by meaning and by keywords) and Semantic Scholar are searched, results merged by DOI/title, the
30 closest by embedding kept, every DOI and retraction status checked; the page lists every search that was run and says that not finding a paper is not proof it does not exist. Nothing is stored.
Live test on three real ideas (glaucoma screening, reference checking, tractor soil sensors): the top results were the relevant papers; the plan takes 7 to 16 s and the search 11 to 14 s.
Known rough edge: records for software and datasets (Zenodo, GitHub) appear next to papers.

## The idea

A scholar, professor or researcher brings an idea (a PDF, Word file, Markdown or text note, or just a paragraph) and a question. The
platform answers with:

1. the papers that matter, each with a **real, verified citation**, where it was found, and a **preview**;
2. how much of the idea already exists in the literature, and **where the gaps are**;
3. an answer to the question itself, grounded in those papers;
4. an honest statement of what was searched and what was not.

The platform stays general (anyone doing research) and research-oriented. It is the same product promise as the rest of Scholar:
no citation is ever generated by a model; every paper shown is a database record we fetched and checked.

## What the research says (and what it means for the design)

| Finding | Source | Design consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The state-of-the-art recipe is: split the idea into **facets** (purpose, mechanism, evaluation, application domain), retrieve candidates from several routes, re-rank them **by facet overlap**, then let an LLM compare the idea with the top papers. Facet re-ranking was the step that mattered: on the "not novel" cases the full system got 89.7%, without facet re-ranking 13.8%. | Literature-Grounded Novelty Assessment of Scientific Ideas (ACL SDP 2025, [arXiv 2506.22026](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.22026v1)); Scideator ([arXiv 2409.14634](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14634)) | Build exactly this pipeline. Caveat: the authors' own small dataset (67 ideas, top 10 papers only, same annotators wrote the examples), so the numbers are indicative, not a guarantee |
| **LLMs are poor judges of novelty by themselves.** Six frontier models had a mean error near 1 point on a 1-5 scale, bunched into classes 3 and 4. LLM judges rate model-made research questions as highly novel while experts disagree ("novelty mirage"). Plausible-sounding reasoning does not mean a correct verdict. | RINoBench ([arXiv 2603.10303](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.10303)); [arXiv 2606.12071](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.12071) | **No single "X% novel / X% published" score.** It would look precise and be wrong. Show a **coverage map** instead: which parts of the idea have close prior work (with the quoted evidence), and which parts we found nothing for. Never say "novel"; say "no close match found in A, B, C" |
| Frontier models invent citations at very high rates (GPT-4o 78 to 90% in one study); a retrieval-grounded system reached expert-level citation accuracy and was published in Nature (Feb 2026). | OpenScholar ([arXiv 2411.14199](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.14199v1)) | Same principle we already follow: retrieve first, cite only what was retrieved, check every quote |
| OpenAlex has a **semantic search** (`search.semantic`): title and abstract embedded with GTE-large (1,024 dims), paragraph-length queries work best (first 2,000 characters), at most 50 results, 1 request per second, key required. | [OpenAlex docs](https://help.openalex.org/api/semantic-search/) | Tested with our key today: the query "using large language models to check whether a research idea is novel" returned SciMON, the Si et al. 100-researcher study, RINoBench and a 2026 novelty-detection paper. This is the main retrieval route |

Not evaluated in this research: Elicit, Consensus, Undermind, SciSpace and similar commercial tools exist in this space. Before any public
claim of being different, compare against them hands-on.

## Pipeline (what we would build)

```
file / text ──► read in the browser (pdf.js; .docx, .md, .txt) ──► private LLM: idea summary + facets
        │                                                              │
        │                                         user sees and EDITS: facets + the exact search queries
        ▼                                                              ▼
  (never leaves the private route)                   retrieval: OpenAlex semantic + keyword, Semantic Scholar, arXiv,
                                                      PubMed, CORE; merged, de-duplicated by DOI
                                                              │
                                              embedding re-rank (bge-m3) → top 30 → facet re-rank (LLM, abstracts only)
                                                              │
                                  per paper: which facets match, with a QUOTE from the abstract (verified in the text)
                                                              │
        coverage map  +  closest prior work  +  gaps  +  grounded answer to the question  +  report export
```

- **The user confirms the search plan first.** The idea is private; the search queries derived from it are sent to public APIs
  (OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, ...). Scholars worry about idea theft, so the screen shows the exact queries and facets before anything
  leaves, and lets the user remove terms. The idea text itself goes only to the private LLM route (Workers AI).
- **Coverage map, not a percentage.** Rows are the idea's facets and combinations; cells show the closest papers and the quoted
  sentence that matches. A facet with no close paper is shown as "nothing found in the sources we searched", with the list of sources and queries.
- **Gaps** come from the map: combinations nobody covers (mechanism A in domain B, where A and B each exist alone), plus limitations
  and future-work sentences in open-access full texts. Model-suggested gaps are labelled "suggestion", and shown with the papers that motivate them.
- **Feasibility** is split. *Literature feasibility* (was this tried, did it work, which data and methods were used) is grounded in the
  retrieved papers. *Practical feasibility* (time, money, skills) is a model opinion and is labelled that way.
- **Previews.** Open-access papers open in our reader (same as uploads) and can be asked questions ("chat with this paper", already on the roadmap).
  Paywalled papers show the abstract and a link only. We never fetch paywalled full text.
- **Verification is built in.** Every paper comes from a registry record, carries its DOI, and is checked for retraction (already built).

## How we would measure it (before anyone relies on it)

1. **Prior-art recall (deterministic, free of opinion).** Take recent papers, write the "idea" from the abstract, hide the paper itself, and check how
   many of the paper's own references (OpenAlex `referenced_works`) appear in the top 10, 30, 50 results. That is a real, repeatable number.
   Target set: 100 papers across fields, including Indian-institution papers, as in our held-out eval.
2. **Quote check:** every evidence quote must appear in the abstract it is attributed to (the same `quoteInText` rule as paper Q&A). Gate: 0 unverified quotes shown.
3. **Calibration check** on RINoBench (public, 1,381 expert-scored ideas): report where we agree with experts and where we do not, instead of hiding it.
4. **Expert spot check:** 20 ideas from pilot scholars, judged by the scholars themselves ("did it miss something you know?").

### Rough cost per check (estimates, to be measured)

- LLM: idea 2 to 5k tokens + about 20 abstracts (6k) + outputs: roughly 15 to 25k tokens. Our daily quota is 60k tokens per user: about 3 checks a day. Revisit the quota or the model when this ships.
- OpenAlex: the free key has a $1 per day budget (we saw 0.001 USD per search); semantic search is a few calls per check. Fine for a pilot, and shared public lookups should be cached.
- Everything else (Workflows, D1, R2, Vectorize at this scale) is inside the Workers Paid plan.

## Risks, said plainly

- "Not found" is not "novel". The databases miss grey literature, theses, many regional-language and non-indexed journals. The report must say so every time.
- Abstract-level comparison misses ideas that are only in full text. Open-access full text helps; paywalled text is out of reach.
- Users will want one number. Resist it; offer the coverage map and a one-line plain-language summary instead.
- The idea is the user's most sensitive data. Private route only, delete on request, never used for training, and the search-plan screen.

## Suggested build order

1. **M1: Related work from an idea** (no model judgment): upload or paste, confirm queries, OpenAlex semantic + keyword + S2, merged and re-ranked, previews, verified citations. Useful on its own and cheap.
2. **M2: facets and coverage map** with quote-verified evidence, plus the prior-art recall eval.
3. **M3: gaps, feasibility, grounded answer, report export**, plus calibration on RINoBench and a pilot with real scholars.


## Sources and kinds of record (22 Oct 2026)

- Searched: OpenAlex (by meaning and by keywords), Semantic Scholar (keywords), PubMed and arXiv (the first two keyword searches each; arXiv is asked one request at a time, 3.1 s apart, as it requests). Each paper lists the searches that found it; a failing source is reported, never hidden.
- Every record gets a **kind** (paper, preprint, book, thesis, report, dataset or software) from OpenAlex's own type; a record with no type that sits in a data repository (Zenodo, figshare, Dryad, OSF ...) or a GitHub-like venue counts as data. Front pages, errata, peer-review reports, grants and supplementary files are dropped.
- Datasets and software are listed after all papers, hidden by default, and never sent to the comparison (they are not prior art the way a paper is). The page says how many are hidden and lets the researcher show them.
- Measured: neither PubMed nor arXiv changes how many of a paper's own references are found (eval README); they are there for recent work.

## M2 as built (22 Oct 2026): compare the closest papers with the idea

After the search, the researcher can press **Compare with my idea**. A private model reads the abstracts of the 8 closest papers (public text we already showed) and, for each paper and each of the four
parts of the idea (purpose, method, testing, field), answers *same / similar / different / not said*. For *same* and *similar* it must quote the abstract.

What is checked and what is not claimed:
- **Every shown match has a quote that was checked to be in that abstract** (the paper-Q&A rule for one continuous piece; for a quote joined with "..." each piece of at least 3 words must be there, in order). A claim whose quote fails is not shown as a match; the page says how many were dropped. Keywords strung together fail on purpose.
- **How close a paper is overall is computed here from the verified parts** (close = three parts match including purpose or method; partly = two; slightly = one). The model never gives it.
- **The coverage map** (one row per part: covered / something similar / not found in these papers / your text does not say) and the "method and field together" line are computed from verified parts only, and say that they describe these 8 papers, not the literature.
- **No score, no "novel"**, and no model-written summary sentence (it could not be checked).
- A quote can still be a weak reason (the model may call "provides accurate results at a lower cost" similar for a field); the researcher sees the sentence and can judge it.

Measured with the real model (gpt-oss-120b on Workers AI), glaucoma-screening idea, 8 papers:
| | tokens in | tokens out | time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| plan | about 400 | about 700 | 8 to 15 s |
| search | none (OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar) | none | 11 to 13 s |
| compare | 3,000 to 3,500 | about 5,700 | 25 to 60 s |
So **a full check is about 10,000 tokens**, and the daily allowance of 60,000 tokens a person allows about **five or six full checks a day**. That is enough for a pilot; raise it (`dailyTokenQuota` in `src/llm/config.ts`) only if people hit it, after looking at real use on `/admin`.
First live run: 11 of about 30 claimed matches were dropped because the model joined single keywords with "..."; after telling it to quote one or two real pieces and checking pieces separately, 4 of about 30 were dropped (no shown match lacks its sentence). The comparison call has a 90 s limit (the gateway's usual 25 s was too short).

## M3 as built (22 Oct 2026): gaps, what was tried, an answer, a report

After the comparison, the researcher sees **gaps**, and can press **Read the papers** for a second reading of the same abstracts. At the end, **Download the report** (Markdown) or print it.

**Gaps** have three kinds, labelled as different things:
1. *No compared paper covers a part of the idea.* From the comparison's coverage rows. No AI.
2. *Two parts each covered, never in the same paper* (for example the method in one paper and the field in another). Every pair of parts is tested; computed from the verified matches only (`combinationGaps`, shared code). No AI, no quota.
3. *What the papers themselves say is still open.* The AI picks sentences from the abstracts that state a limitation, a challenge or future work; each is shown only if it is in the abstract AND contains a cue word of that kind (`limit`, `future`, `remains`, `next step`, `need to`, ...). Real abstracts rarely contain such a sentence: in three real ideas it gave 0 to 2 a time, and the page says "none of the abstracts states one" when there is none. Model-invented "suggested gaps" are NOT included: they cannot be checked.

**The second reading** (`POST /api/v1/idea/insights`, same safeguards as the comparison: sign-in, same origin, privacy note, size limit, private route, nothing stored) reads only the papers that matched some part of the idea (an unrelated paper's result says nothing about this idea) and returns four things:
- *What each paper reports*: worked / mixed / did not work / not stated, **with the sentence**; plus a short quote naming the method and one naming the data. An outcome without a verified sentence is "not stated".
- *An answer to the researcher's question.* The evidence is a sentence of an abstract, verified to be there. The model's own one-line wording is shown only if the sentence supports it: every number in it must be in the sentence, and more than half of its other content words (without numbers). Otherwise the sentence is shown alone as "a sentence the AI picked for your question". If nothing verifies, the page says the abstracts do not answer it.
- *Practical feasibility* (time, cost, skills): the model's opinion, up to 3 notes, in a box labelled "the AI's opinion, not checked against any paper". Any note that says the idea is new or has been done is not shown (we never say that either way).
- Claims whose sentence is not in the abstract are dropped and counted ("The AI also made N claims whose sentence is not in the abstract"). The shape of verification is the same as M2; the numbers are in the roadmap.

**The report** is built in the browser from what is on the page (`buildIdeaReport`, shared code, tested): the idea as the plan states it, what was searched (and which searches failed), the closest papers with DOI links (no datasets), the coverage, the gaps, what the papers report with quotes, the answer, the opinion in its own labelled section, and a fixed "what this report cannot tell you" (theses, books and regional journals are not covered; abstracts only; the model can misread). Everything from a database or a model is escaped so it cannot add links, headings or HTML. It never says whether an idea is new. Nothing is sent anywhere to make it.

So the daily allowance of 60,000 tokens a person is **two to three whole checks** (the comparison alone is about an eighth of it, the second reading about a fifth). Cloudflare's published price for this model (about $0.35 per million tokens in and $0.75 out; not re-checked today) makes a whole check roughly 1 to 2 cents. Decide the allowance before the pilot (roadmap, D6). The second reading's time limit is 150 s (the comparison's is 90 s).

What the claim checks did on real abstracts (they work as designed, and they cost yield):
- The model often changes a word of a quote ("disc-centred" for "disc-centered"), or copies 80% of a long sentence: such a claim is dropped (2 to 13 per reading). Asking it to copy exactly and to prefer short pieces helped a little; it varies from run to run.
- Its one-line statements often add a figure the sentence does not contain ("208 adults", "63"): that is exactly what the figure check is for, and the sentence is then shown alone.
- A method or data quote may be a short phrase (2 words or more) when it is in the abstract word for word; a sentence about a result needs 5 words or more.
- Not yet measured: whether people find the gaps useful. That is the pilot's job (roadmap I5).
